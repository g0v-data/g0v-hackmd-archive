# What "Acellular and Non-DNA" Means, and Why It Matters

Most product descriptions tell you what's inside. "Acellular" and "non-DNA" tell you what's been left out. Both terms show up often in regenerative science, so they're worth understanding for anyone reading about the field, including readers who've come across the Regenerative Protein Array (RPA) by Genesis Regenerative. One term means there are no living cells. The other means there's no DNA detected under the testing method used. They aren't the same thing, and the difference matters.

"Acellular" means there are no intact, living cells. That matters because every cell carries an ID badge. Proteins on the cell's surface, called HLA or MHC molecules, help the immune system tell "self" from "not self." Donor Stem cells were once thought to slip past the recipient's immune system. Researchers now know that isn't always true. In some cases, the immune system recognizes and attacks donor cells, and some people develop antibodies against them. Living cells also need careful storage to stay alive, a concern that doesn’t apply in the same way to acellular products, although those products still have their own storage requirements.

"Non-DNA" addresses a separate question. Even with no intact cells, a product could still contain bits of donor genetic material left over from processing. Researchers have set benchmarks for assessing residual DNA in certain decellularized tissues. For processed tissue, one widely cited set of criteria has three parts. There should be less than 50 nanograms of double-stranded DNA per milligram of dry material. Any remaining fragments should be shorter than 200 base pairs. And no cell nuclei should be visible under a microscope. Those numbers were written for tissue scaffolds, not protein products, but the question behind them is the same: how much donor DNA is left?

Here's where the two terms part ways. "Cell-free" doesn't automatically mean "DNA-free." Extracellular vesicles are a good example. Cells release them, but they have no nucleus and are unable to copy themselves, so they aren't cells. Yet researchers have found fragments of DNA inside some vesicles and on the surface of others. A product may be fully acellular and still carry genetic material unless it has been specifically processed and tested to remove it.

That's why it helps to look at the two terms separately. A product should have test results showing it contains no intact cells, plus separate results showing residual DNA was detected and, if so, how much was measured. When evaluating any regenerative product, the practical questions are simple. How was each one verified? By what method? By which laboratory? Third-party test results, including sterility testing and measurements of protein and DNA content, are what turn a label into something a clinician is able to check.

In short, "acellular" and "non-DNA" are two separate statements, and each one should have its own test results behind it.
