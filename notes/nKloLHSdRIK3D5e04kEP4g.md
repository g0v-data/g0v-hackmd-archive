<h2>Regulatory basis</h2>
<p>The compliance adviser points to core statutes first. The Gambling Act 2005 remains the primary law that gives the Commission its licensing powers and oversight across Great Britain, and the Commission uses licence conditions to translate those powers into operational rules for remote casinos and betting sites. One specific licence condition addresses payments, and that condition requires licence holders to accept payments only through providers that meet the Payment Services Regulations 2017 definition, which the Commission referenced using SI 2017 No. 752. The change to how payment services must be qualified is part of the Commission&rsquo;s broader approach to reduce harm and tighten control over transaction channels.</p>
<p>LCCP Condition 5.1.2 is the focused regulatory lever. The Commission introduced that condition to ensure payment routes are traceable and meet financial rules that sit alongside gambling protections, and the condition took effect on 31 January 2024 to give firms time to align their payment chains with the statutory definition. The compliance expert emphasises that the condition is not a technicality: it shifts responsibility to licensed operators to use compliant providers and to manage risk at the point a consumer tries to add funds. The practical outcome is closer oversight where money moves into gambling accounts.</p>

<p>The compliance specialist uses the local term. Telephone billing or phone bill refers to deposits charged to a mobile or landline, and industry writers usually call it carrier-billing or bill-to-phone. Such a deposit appears as a charge on the consumer&rsquo;s phone bill or is deducted from prepaid mobile credit, and operators must decide whether to accept that channel under the Commission&rsquo;s payment rules. The regulatory focus is on who processes the payment and whether that provider meets the Payment Services Regulations 2017 definition.</p>
<p>Phone-linked methods can look simple to consumers. A short on-screen flow, a confirmation and a charge placed on the mobile account may complete the payment, but the compliance expert warns that apparent ease does not reduce regulatory duties on operators to verify the payment route and the customer. Firms must be able to evidence that the payment provider is subject to the rules in SI 2017 No. 752, and that any customer protections required by the Commission are enforced at the point of deposit. Operators who cannot demonstrate that are exposed to enforcement action.</p>
<h2>Player protection mechanism explained</h2>
<p>The compliance expert explains one concrete protection. When a customer self-excludes through GAMSTOP, the operator must close the account and return any money held in it, remove the customer from marketing lists, and take reasonable steps to prevent any attempt to gamble during the exclusion period. GAMSTOP is the national online self-exclusion scheme for Great Britain and the Commission requires all gambling businesses to participate, which makes the measure a central protective mechanism. The expert lays out how that mechanism works in practice and how it links to payment handling.</p>
<p>GAMSTOP updates its list of self-excluded consumers every 24 hours, and operators must check that list to avoid processing deposits or allowing play for excluded individuals, which means account systems and payment gateways must be linked into the screening process so that a deposit attempt triggers a block before funds arrive. The Commission describes self-exclusion as a formal agreement not to gamble, and it expects firms to take the technical and operational steps that demonstrate reasonable effort to stop a customer who tries to play. The effect is that a successful self-exclusion should stop the commercial flow at the account level and prevent a payment from being accepted.</p>
<h2>Affordability and checks</h2>
<p>The compliance adviser notes standard checks. Affordability checks have become a normal part of the operator&rsquo;s toolkit to assess whether a customer can afford to gamble as they intend, and those checks may be proportionate according to play patterns, spend and operator risk indicators. The Commission&rsquo;s supervisory approach expects firms to apply checks that match the level of risk a customer presents, and to record the checks and decisions taken. The expert emphasises that affordability work sits alongside self-exclusion and payment controls.</p>
<p>Checks may involve reviewing transaction history and payment behaviour, and they should be targeted rather than universal fingerprinting that has no analytic basis, so operators must keep records that justify any intervention. The compliance expert points out that when a deposit route is phone billing, the operator needs to consider whether the phone account holder is the account holder of the gambling account and whether the payment provider can validate identity and authorisation in a way that meets the Commission&rsquo;s expectations. Those steps form part of the operator&rsquo;s duty to detect harm and act on it.</p>

<h2>Operational responsibilities for operators</h2>
<p>The compliance expert lists the operator&rsquo;s core duties. Licensed firms must show that their payment service providers meet the definition in the Payment Services Regulations 2017, and they must evidence that the provider is suitable under LCCP Condition 5.1.2. Firms must also integrate self-exclusion checks such as GAMSTOP into onboarding and deposit screening so that excluded customers cannot place funds into an account. The regulator expects accurate record keeping to support these controls and to show why an operator made a particular decision.</p>
<p>Operators carry the burden of proof in an investigation. The compliance adviser points out that the Commission will examine contracting with PSPs, the technical links to self-exclusion screens, the workflow for affordability checks, and the steps taken to remove a customer from marketing lists after a self-exclusion agreement. The expert stresses that simply naming a PSP is not enough; the licence holder must demonstrate ongoing oversight and audit capability so that compliance is demonstrable in regulatory review. That is a continuous governance duty rather than a one-off compliance box.</p>
<h2>Interaction with self-exclusion and support lines</h2>
<p>The compliance specialist emphasises GAMSTOP as central. GAMSTOP is the national online self-exclusion scheme for Great Britain and every regulated online gambling business must be registered to use it, which creates a common tool for consumers who wish to stop gambling across operators. GAMSTOP updates its list of self-excluded consumers every 24 hours, which obliges operators to check the data feed regularly so that a newly excluded person is stopped from gambling. The regulator frames self-exclusion as a formal agreement that operators must respect and operationalise in their systems.</p>
<p>The scheme provides consumer contact facilities and helplines that operators should reference in support material, and GAMSTOP offers a contact line at 0800 138 6518 available 10am to 8pm, seven days a week, while GAMSTOP BETTING SHOPS provides a free helpline number at 0800 294 2060 for betting-shop related exclusions. The compliance expert highlights that operators must close accounts where a self-exclusion is in place, return any funds in the account, and remove the customer from marketing databases the firm uses. These steps are non-negotiable elements of the Commission&rsquo;s player protection framework.</p>
<h2>Case handling and dispute resolution</h2>
<p>The compliance adviser outlines expected handling. Where a customer claims a phone-linked deposit bypassed self-exclusion or that an affordability check was not applied, the operator must investigate, retain evidence of the payment chain and the screening checks, and provide a clear response that shows the actions taken. The regulator reviews how promptly the firm closes accounts and refunds money where self-exclusion applies, and it looks for documented processes that match the action taken. The expert recommends that records be organised to allow an auditable trail.</p>
<p>Dispute resolution should also include clear escalation pathways for customers and regulators, and firms should note that poor record keeping or inconsistent application of checks increases regulatory risk and consumer harm. The compliance expert suggests that operators maintain a staged response plan that logs key decision points in a deposit and account closure scenario so that any regulatory query can be answered with documentary proof of policies, checks and outcomes. That practice aligns with the Commission&rsquo;s expectation of transparent governance.</p>
